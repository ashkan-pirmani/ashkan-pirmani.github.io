Where the evidence comes from, and why it is hard to get

Two things have to be true before a health question can be answered. There have to be enough records, and you have to be allowed to use them. Usually one of the two fails. This page is the plain version of both. The projects themselves are on the home page.

01Real world data

From records to evidence

Most real world evidence does not work this way, and that is fine. A company licenses a big database, it sits in one place, and it is already legal to query. Try that first. It runs out in three places. When the patients are rare. When they are outside the United States. When the thing you want to measure was never written into a claim. That is where my work starts.

Start here. What ordinary care leaves behind, not what a trial sets out to collect. The field calls it real world data.

Visits, laboratory results, imaging and prescriptions arrive on their own schedule. Which is to say, on no schedule. Each source alone gives a weak answer, and no two of them agree.

Put them together and one answer holds across all of them. Getting from the top of this picture to the bottom is what the field calls real world evidence. That is the part I spend my time on.

The difficultyWhat I do about it

Every source records things its own way.
Two registries can disagree on what a visit is. Making them line up is most of the work.
It was never collected for research.
Gaps and bias are built in. So the quality checks belong inside the pipeline, not in a caveat at the end of a paper.
Rare conditions leave every cohort short.
No single place has enough patients to see a pattern. The only route is to combine what each of them knows separately.

02Federated learning

The model moves, the records stay

This is how I get at it. One model, trained across many separate places at once. The data never leaves home. The name for it is federated learning. It is a method, not a field I belong to.

The records. They never leave the place that holds them. The model. It visits each one and learns there.

More precisely: every place trains its own copy on its own records, and sends back only what the copy learned. Those are averaged into one model, and that model goes out again. That round repeats. The records are never part of what is sent.

The difficultyWhat I do about it

No two places hold the same kind of patient.
One shared model fits none of them well. I let it adapt to each place, while it still learns from all of them. That is what closed the gap above.
Most groups are offered one deal, take it or leave it.
So I made the in between an option. A group picks how far it goes, and moves along as trust grows. Nobody has to walk away.
Federation alone is not privacy.
The records stay home, but gradients can leak, and membership inference is real. So I treat the governance agreement, and whatever you add on top, as part of the method rather than paperwork around it. Federated does not mean safe on its own, and there is a large attack literature that says so.
Most teams have nobody to install it.
So the tooling has to arrive with that already solved. I treat it as part of the design, not as something the site is expected to supply.

03Approach

What I hold to

Four things I do not bend on. They decide what I build, and what I refuse to build.

Privacy rules are the specification, not the obstacle.

If a method only works once the rules are relaxed, it is not a method. It is a wish. I start from what is allowed, and build inside it.

If a site needs its own engineer to begin, it will never begin.

The groups who need this most cannot hire an engineer for one study. Making that unnecessary is the engineering, not a shortcut around it. I judge my own work by whether a small group can run it without me in the room.

Evidence that never leaves the paper changes no care.

Patients, clinicians and funders belong in the work, not in the audience at the end. That is why nearly everything I build is open.

Messy data is still data.

Ordinary care records are incomplete and biased. They are still the only trace most people leave behind. Throwing them away is easy. Handling them well is the job.

04Before this

How I got here

I did not start in health. I started in parcels.

Before any of this I spent two years working out where Iran Post should put its sorting hubs. I built a simulation of the national network and searched it for a better layout. The scenario it found came out 15% lower on projected transport cost, on paper and never in the road network. I turned it into a decision support dashboard and put it in front of the executives who decide. Moving parcels and moving models turn out to be the same problem. You are always deciding what travels and what stays.

It is also why I am hard to lose in a room where nobody shares a first language. Which is most of the rooms this work happens in.
